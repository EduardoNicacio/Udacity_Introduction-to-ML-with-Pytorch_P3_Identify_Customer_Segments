## 1. Elevator Pitch

**The Problem:** A mail-order sales company in Germany needs to know which segments of the general population form its core customer base, so that marketing spend can be aimed at households with the highest expected response rate. The raw material is two 85-column demographic datasets from Bertelsmann Arvato Analytics: 891,221 people from the general German population (AZDIAS) and 191,652 mail-order customers - both semicolon-delimited, both riddled with sentinel-coded missing values (0, -1, 'X', 'XX', 'XX' strings, and naturally-blank cells), mixed-type features, and string categoricals that no clustering algorithm can consume as-is.

**The Solution:** A full unsupervised-learning pipeline in `Identify_Customer_Segments.ipynb`: parse every `missing_or_unknown` code in the 85-row feature summary into real NaNs (with a custom parser for the unquoted `[-1,X]` / `[XX]` entries that break `ast.literal_eval`), drop six columns missing more than 20% of their values, trim rows with more than 15 missing values, re-encode binary categoricals (OST_WEST_KZ 'W'/'O' → 1/0), drop 13 multi-level categoricals, and engineer four clean features from two mixed-type originals (PRAEGENDE_JUGENDJAHRE → DECADE + MOVEMENT; CAMEO_INTL_2015 → WEALTH + LIFE_STAGE). A reusable `clean_data()` function applies the identical transformation to customer data. The cleaned 792,287 × 66 matrix is median-imputed, standardized, reduced with PCA to 30 components (87.47% of variance), and clustered with k-means (k = 10, chosen at the elbow). The fitted imputer, scaler, PCA, and k-means objects - never re-fit - then transform and assign every customer.

**The Result:** Customer share versus population share exposes the business answer directly: **cluster 3 (3.67× over-represented: 6.8% of the population → 24.8% of customers)** profiles as older, high-income, financially active, green-avantgarde men, and the **set-aside high-missingness segment (label –1, 2.39×: 11.1% → 26.5%)** shows that customer *records* far more often exceed the row-missingness threshold - a data-completeness difference, not established respondent behavior. The anti-segments are just as clear: **cluster 4 (0.12×)** dreamful, non-investing young women of the 1980s–90s, **cluster 5 (0.09×)** financially-disengaged young women, and **clusters 1/8 (0.10×/0.19×)** event-oriented men - clusters 1, 4, 5 and 8 together are ~33.7% of the population but only ~4.1% of customers. The notebook executes top-to-bottom with **0 error outputs** (34 code cells executed, 6 embedded figures), every Discussion cell quoting measured numbers, and an HTML export ready for submission.

### Key Design Decisions

| Decision | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Custom `parse_missing_codes()` instead of bare `ast.literal_eval`** | `feat_info` stores codes like `[-1,X]` and `[XX]` *unquoted*, so `literal_eval` raises `ValueError: malformed node`. The parser tries `literal_eval` first, then falls back to strip-and-split with int coercion - one function, used by both the manual AZDIAS pass and `clean_data()` |
| **Numeric-vs-string comparison branch in the NaN loop** | After `read_csv` on pandas 3.0, `OST_WEST_KZ` and the three CAMEO columns are `str`-dtype while others are numeric. For numeric columns the code is compared directly (`values == code`); for everything else the comparison is stringified (`values.astype(str) == str(code)`) so `-1` matches `'-1'` and `0` never accidentally matches `'0'` |
| **20% column threshold → 6 columns** | The histogram is bimodal: most columns <5% missing, six far right (34.8%–99.8%). 20% cleanly separates the modes and matches the gap between ALTER_HH (34.8%) and KKK/REGIOTYP (17.7%) |
| **15 missing values per row** | The row distribution is bimodal with an empty valley: 80.8% of rows have ≤5 missing, a second mode sits at 34–49, and only ~0.7% of rows fall in 16–32. Any threshold in 15–32 gives the same split; 15 is the conservative round number inside the valley |
| **Drop 13 multi-level categoricals instead of one-hot encoding** | One-hot encoding would add ~120 dummy columns (90 from CAMEO_DEU_2015 alone). In a distance-based pipeline (PCA + k-means) that many sparse 0/1 columns would dominate the Euclidean geometry. Several dropped features are also coarse duplicates (LP_FAMILIE_GROB ⊂ LP_FAMILIE_FEIN) or overlap with engineered WEALTH/LIFE_STAGE |
| **Engineer DECADE/MOVEMENT and WEALTH/LIFE_STAGE with explicit dictionaries** | Both mixed features encode multiple concepts in one code. Dictionary `.map()` is deterministic, auditable, and keeps the ordinal structure (WEALTH 1–5) that a dummy encoding would destroy. CAMEO digit-splitting is NaN-safe because `to_numeric(errors='coerce')` maps 'nan' back to NaN |
| **Keep WOHNAGE and PLZ8_BAUMAX despite "mixed" label** | `feat_info` flags them mixed, but after sentinel conversion they are pure integer ordinals with no string levels - the simplifying "ordinals act as intervals" assumption applies; dropping them would discard legitimate neighborhood signal |
| **`clean_data()` references outer-scope variables (`feat_info`-derived `missing_codes`, `cols_to_drop`, `multi_level_cats`, the maps)** | The rubric requires one cleaning path applied identically to both datasets. Reusing the exact fitted-state variables (not re-deriving them) guarantees customer data cannot silently diverge from population data |
| **Median imputation, not mean or row-drop** | Count features (ANZ_HAUSHALTE_AKTIV, ANZ_TITEL) are right-skewed; mean imputation would be pulled by tails. Dropping rows with any NaN would discard the 21% of clean rows that still carry a gap. Median is robust and keeps every row |
| **PCA n_components = 30 (87.47%)** | Inside the prescribed 80–90% band: 22 components reach 80%, 34 reach 90%. 30 is the round number that captures ~87.5% while more than halving 66 dimensions - enough granularity for k-means, compact enough to limit distance concentration |
| **One quality contract: `QUALITY_COLUMNS` + `assess_row_quality()`** | Round 1 measured customer row quality over the 66-column *engineered* frame while the population used the 79-column *pre-engineering* frame - same threshold, different input, which reviewers rejected. Now `QUALITY_COLUMNS = azdias.columns.tolist()` (79 cols, post-1.1.2, pre-1.2) is defined at step 1.1.3, and `assess_row_quality()` re-runs the missing-code conversion and sums over exactly that list *before* any engineering; the boolean mask keeps the original customer index and is applied with label-aligned `.loc` (asserted: retained ∩ set-aside = ∅, union = all 191,652 rows) |
| **Reuse `ROW_MISSING_THRESHOLD` for customers + `OUTLIER_LABEL = -1` in proportions** | The step-1.1.3 threshold is the project's single definition of "complete" data, so step 3.2 re-applies the *same* variable to customers instead of inventing a second rule (140,898 kept, 50,754 set aside). Label `-1` cannot collide with k-means ids 0-9, letting the set-aside group appear in the proportion table as a visible segment while `describe_cluster()` skips it (no centroid exists) |
| **k_final = 10 at the elbow, random_state=42, n_init=10** | The average within-cluster distance drops steeply to k≈8, then <1.0 unit per additional cluster (k10→11 saves 0.75; k14→15 saves 0.31). 10 sits just past the elbow; the fixed seed and n_init make the 3.67×/0.12×/0.09× ratios fully reproducible |
| **Column alignment `customers_clean[azdias_final.columns]` before transform** | Defensive contract: even though `clean_data()` produces the same 66 columns, explicitly reindexing means a future column-order drift would raise or self-correct instead of silently scrambling the imputer's feature order |
| **Every discussion number written from executed (or dry-run-verified) output** | Discussion cells quote 99.8%/77.0%/..., 792,287/98,934, 87.47%, 34.94, 3.67× - all read from printed output, never estimated from charts |

### Why Not

- **`ast.literal_eval` alone?** It crashes on three of the eight non-trivial entries (`[-1,X]`, `[XX]`, `[-1,XX]`). The fallback parser costs ten lines and removes the crash class entirely.
- **One-hot encode multi-level categoricals?** ~120 extra sparse columns would dominate PCA loadings and k-means distances; the project explicitly allows dropping them "to keep things straightforward," and the dropped features' information is largely recoverable from retained ordinals.
- **Keep rows with 34–49 missing values via aggressive imputation?** That group is missing entire survey modules (MOBI_REGIO absent in 99.7% of them) *and* is demographically distinct (younger, wealthier, more socially-minded). Imputing 47 of 79 columns per row would fabricate profiles; dropping them (with an explicit caveat in Discussion 3.3) is the honest choice.
- **Fit `clean_data`-derived objects on customers?** The project's core question is "how do customers map onto population-defined segments?" - re-fitting on customers would redefine the segments and answer a different question. Every sklearn object in Step 3 is transform/predict-only.
- **k-means on the raw 66 features instead of the PCA scores?** Correlated feature blocks (PLZ8_*, KBA05_*, SEMIO_*) would double-count neighborhood and personality axes in the distance metric; the 30 decorrelated components weight each information source once.
- **Agglomerative / DBSCAN / GMM instead of k-means?** On ~800k × 30 dense data, only k-means runs the k=2..15 sweep in minutes; the elbow method is the prescribed selection tool, and the business deliverable (cluster profiles) does not require soft memberships.

## 3. Pipeline Design Principles in Practice

### The Baseline Floor - Measure Before You Model

```txt
┌───────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Customer base = population?  → ratio 1.0  │
│ Thresholds:  > 1.5 over-represented       │
│              < 0.6 under-represented      │
└───────────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                        │ compare customer share to population share
                        ▼
   cluster 3    6.8% pop  → 24.8% cust   ratio 3.67  ← core segment
   segment -1  11.1% pop  → 26.5% cust   ratio 2.39  ← set-aside (over)
   cluster 4   10.0% pop  →  1.2% cust   ratio 0.12  ← anti-segment
   cluster 5    9.2% pop  →  0.8% cust   ratio 0.09  ← anti-segment
```

The null hypothesis is proportionality (ratio ≈ 1.0); the 1.5/0.6 thresholds were fixed *before* looking at the results, so the over/under labels are decisions against a stated bar, not post-hoc story-telling. The ratio plot (notebook §3.3) shows clusters 2 (1.36) and 9 (1.39) hovering near - deliberately not called "winners" - while cluster 3 and the set-aside segment (-1, 2.39) stand far clear of the line.

### Reproducibility by Contract

| Knob | Value | Why |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| Missing-code parsing | deterministic function of `feat_info` | Same codes → same NaNs on any dataset |
| Column/row thresholds | 20% / 15 missing | Fixed in the plan before the pipeline ran; justified by bimodality, not tuned to results |
| Feature engineering | explicit `DECADE_MAP` / `MOVEMENT_MAP` / `OST_WEST_MAP` dicts | Auditable one-line lookup; no regex, no inference |
| Imputer / Scaler / PCA | fitted once on AZDIAS, reused on CUSTOMERS | The comparison is only valid if both datasets share one transform |
| KMeans | `random_state=42`, `n_init=10` | Cluster ids and ratios are byte-stable across runs (verified twice: dry-run and executed notebook agreed exactly) |
| Environment | Python 3.14.7, pandas 3.0.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1, numpy 2.5.1, matplotlib 3.11.2, seaborn 0.13.2 | Recorded so a reviewer can reproduce every printed number |

### Evaluation Hygiene (Single Responsibility per Data Split)

```txt
azdias        ──►  manual clean path → azdias_final → fit imputer/scaler/pca/kmeans
azdias_clean  ──►  792,287 rows with ≤ 15 missing (population row split here)
customers     ──►  assess_row_quality (79 QUALITY_COLUMNS) → mask → clean_data()
              ──►  140,898 rows (50,754 set aside as label -1) → transform-only chain → labels
kmeans.cluster_centers_  ──►  inverse_transform  ──►  interpretable profiles
```

The manual path (Steps 1.1–1.2) and `clean_data()` (Step 1.3) share the same variables by construction, so the 66 customer columns match the 66 population columns exactly (asserted in-cell via `customers_clean[azdias_final.columns]`). Row trimming is explicitly *not* part of `clean_data()` - it belongs to the analysis steps, and the docstring says so. Step 3.2 then repeats the *same* decision through `assess_row_quality()`: missing-code conversion over the identical `QUALITY_COLUMNS` list, pre-engineering, same `ROW_MISSING_THRESHOLD`, mask applied to the cleaned rows by original index (disjointness and full coverage asserted): 140,898 customer rows enter the fitted pipeline and 50,754 (26.5%) are set aside alongside their 98,934 population counterparts, then both set-aside groups re-enter step 3.3 as label -1.

## 6. Challenges & Resolutions

### Challenge 1: `ast.literal_eval` Cannot Parse the Feature Summary

**Symptom:** The first conversion attempt died with `ValueError: malformed node or string on line 1: Name(id='X')` on the very first string-typed entry.

**Root Cause:** `AZDIAS_Feature_Summary.csv` stores missing codes as `[-1,X]`, `[XX]`, and `[-1,XX]` - the string codes are *unquoted*, which is not valid Python literal syntax. Three of the 85 rows are affected (CAMEO_DEUG_2015, CAMEO_DEU_2015, CAMEO_INTL_2015) - exactly the columns the brief singles out for special handling.

**Fix:** `parse_missing_codes()` tries `ast.literal_eval` first (keeping clean parsing for the 70+ `[-1,0]`-style entries), and on failure strips brackets, splits on commas, strips quotes, and coerces tokens to `int` when possible. The result is a dict `missing_codes: attribute → list` consumed by both the manual AZDIAS pass and `clean_data()`.

**Takeaway:** Metadata files are code-adjacent but not code. Probe the parser against the *real* file in the first five minutes - this failure would otherwise surface as a mid-notebook traceback after a 3-second CSV load.

### Challenge 2: A Seaborn-Free Environment and Pandas 3.0 String Columns

**Symptom:** `import seaborn` failed (`ModuleNotFoundError`); separately, `azdias.dtypes` showed four columns as pandas 3.0's new `str` dtype (`OST_WEST_KZ`, `CAMEO_DEUG_2015`, `CAMEO_DEU_2015`, `CAMEO_INTL_2015`), and missing-code replacement must compare `-1` (an `int` from `feat_info`) against `'-1'` (a string in the column).

**Root Cause:** The base image ships Jupyter + sklearn but not seaborn; pandas 3.0 infers strings as `str` dtype by default, changing comparison semantics from the object-dtype behavior older notebooks assume.

**Fix:** `pip install seaborn` (0.13.2), then a 5-line probe confirmed `countplot` works on pandas 3.0. The NaN loop branches on dtype: numeric columns compare raw codes, everything else compares `values.astype(str) == str(code)`. Verified end-to-end: 3,477,091 values converted, CAMEO 'X'/'XX' gone, OST_WEST_KZ mapping clean.

**Takeaway:** Install-and-probe beats assume-and-debug. The dtype branch is uglier than a single comparison, but it is the difference between "converted 3.5M codes" and "silently converted 0."

### Challenge 3: `countplot` + Hue Does Not Normalize Per Subset

**Symptom:** The 1.1.3 comparison needs distribution shapes for 792,287 clean rows vs. 98,934 outlier rows. A single `countplot(x=col, hue=subset, stat='percent')` probe returned 40/20/40% bars - normalization by the *global* total, so the 8× larger clean subset would crush the outlier bars into invisibility.

**Root Cause:** seaborn 0.13's `stat='percent'` divides by the grand total across the figure, not within each hue group.

**Fix:** A 6-row × 2-column grid of countplots (one panel per column per subset, the layout the brief literally describes: "seaborn's countplot() and matplotlib's subplot() to put bar charts for the two subplots side by side"), with per-row `ylim` alignment so magnitude *and* shape are both readable. A numeric summary table (means + %missing) printed underneath backs the visual.

**Takeaway:** A 10-second semantics probe on the exact plot call prevents a figure that is technically compliant and analytically useless.

### Challenge 4: Writing Discussions That Quote Numbers Before the Notebook Runs

**Symptom:** Every Discussion cell must contain "real observations - not placeholder text," but the executed outputs do not exist until after the code is written.

**Root Cause:** Classic ordering problem: prose depends on outputs; outputs depend on code; reviews depend on prose.

**Fix:** A three-act sequence: (a) read-only dry-runs of the *identical* pipeline on the real CSVs, capturing every number a Discussion would need; (b) write code + discussions together, quoting dry-run figures; (c) execute the notebook and cross-check each quoted number against the executed output. Act (c) found two errors (outlier median 43 vs. the 47 I had written; 21% vs. "~10%" of clean rows retaining gaps) - both patched in markdown via `nbformat` without re-execution, because markdown edits do not invalidate code outputs.

**Takeaway:** Dry-run first, quote second, verify third. The verification pass caught a 100% error rate on the two numbers I had *not* directly transcribed from printed output - a small lesson with a clear boundary.

### Challenge 5: The Elbow Loop Will Kill the Default Cell Timeout

**Symptom:** Timing probes showed one `KMeans(n_clusters=10, n_init=10)` fit ≈ 8 s and the full k=2..15 sweep ≈ 104 s on 792,287 × 30 data. `jupyter nbconvert --execute` defaults to a 30-second per-cell timeout.

**Root Cause:** The default timeout exists for interactive convenience; this notebook's heaviest cell is a 14-fit sweep the brief explicitly prescribes.

**Fix:** Executed with `--ExecutePreprocessor.timeout=1800`. The elbow cell completed in ~104 s; total notebook execution ~6 minutes including the 150 MB CSV load. PCA, notably, was cheap (0.2 s for the full 66-component fit) because scikit-learn 1.9's `auto` solver selects `covariance_eigh` for wide matrices - a pleasant surprise the dry-run surfaced before any timing promise was made.

**Takeaway:** Time the expensive cell in rehearsal, then size the timeout from the measurement with headroom. Never let a framework default decide whether your prescribed analysis is allowed to finish.

### Challenge 6: Segment Labels Must Not Be Story-Telling

**Symptom:** The ratio analysis makes it tempting to narrate every cluster with a catchy persona, including the middle band (ratios 0.64–1.39) that is neither clearly over- nor under-represented.

**Root Cause:** Business narratives reward confidence; the pre-committed 1.5/0.6 thresholds exist precisely to resist that pull.

**Fix:** Only clusters crossing the thresholds get profile paragraphs in Discussion 3.3 (over: 3 plus the set-aside -1 segment; under: 1, 4, 5, 8). Clusters 0, 6, 7, 2, 9 appear in the proportion table and the chart, with 2 (1.36) and 9 (1.39) noted as *near* - deliberately not claimed. The recommendation names the single core cluster, the four anti-segments, and the set-aside segment (shares but no centroid) as the standing caveat.

**Takeaway:** Pre-register the decision rule, then report against it. The interesting honesty is in what the analysis declines to conclude.

### Challenge 7: `clean_data()` Must Be the Manual Path, Bit-for-Bit

**Symptom:** The rubric requires one cleaning function applied to customer data; the notebook builds `azdias_final` through a *manual* sequence of cells (1.1.1–1.2.3). Two paths, one truth.

**Root Cause:** Educational notebooks teach by expansion (show every step), while the rubric demands contraction (one reusable function). If the two paths drift - a special case applied in one but not the other - the customer transform silently misaligns.

**Fix:** `clean_data()` consumes the *same outer-scope variables* the manual path created (`missing_codes`, `cols_to_drop`, `multi_level_cats`, `DECADE_MAP`, `MOVEMENT_MAP`, `OST_WEST_MAP`) rather than re-deriving its own copies, and applies the identical special-case substitutions. The Step 3.2 cell then asserts alignment by reindexing `customers_clean[azdias_final.columns]` before the first transform. Executed evidence: `clean_data()` yields (191652, 66), and after the step-3.2 row split the pipeline consumes (140898, 66) - identical column count and names, with the mask's union provably equal to the original customer index.

**Takeaway:** When a notebook must contain both a demonstration path and a production path, make the production path a *function of the demonstration's artifacts*. Shared state is the cheapest consistency guarantee available.

## 10. Retrospective

### What I'd Do Differently

1. **Transcribe, never recall.** The only two errors found in verification (outlier median 47→43; "~10%"→21%) were numbers written from memory of the dry-run rather than copied from the printed line. A hard rule - "if I cannot point at the output line, the sentence does not ship" - would have made the patch pass a no-op.
2. **Rehearse the customer chain in the same script as the population chain.** The first customer dry-run forgot the `pca.transform` step before `kmeans.predict` (a 66-vs-30 feature error caught in seconds, but avoidable). One script that runs *both* sides end-to-end would have exercised the transform-only contract before the notebook existed.
3. **Install seaborn during recon, not after planning.** The plan correctly listed it as step 1, but the environment probe identified the gap *during* recon - installing immediately would have allowed the countplot design to be tested in the same session as the probe.
4. **Print the row-threshold sensitivity table in the notebook.** The "rows > 5 / > 8 / > 10 / > 15 / > 20" counts were computed in dry-run and quoted in Discussion 1.1.3, but the notebook only shows the threshold-15 split. One more print would let a reviewer verify the valley argument without rerunning anything.
5. **Bind the ratio thresholds as named constants.** 1.5 and 0.6 appear as literals in the plot code and the discussion. Storing `OVER_THRESHOLD = 1.5` / `UNDER_THRESHOLD = 0.6` and printing the flagged cluster lists from them would make the pre-registration visible in code, not just in prose.

### What Worked Well

1. **Read-only dry-runs as the planning instrument.** Every threshold, component count, cluster count, and discussion number in the approved plan came from executing the real pipeline in throwaway scripts. The notebook's job became "reproduce the rehearsal," not "discover the analysis."
2. **Fixing the parser before writing the notebook.** `parse_missing_codes()` was proven against the actual `feat_info` strings in minute one; the notebook never saw a parsing traceback.
3. **Determinism as a contract.** `random_state=42` + `n_init=10` + shared preprocessing variables made the dry-run and the executed notebook agree exactly on shapes, inertias, and ratios - turning verification into a diff instead of a debate.
4. **nbformat-based filling with structural asserts.** One reviewed generator script replaced 25 fragile hand edits to `.ipynb` JSON; asserts on cell types meant a miscounted index would fail loudly before writing.
5. **Markdown-only patches after execution.** The two corrected numbers were fixed with `nbformat` cell-source edits that leave code outputs untouched - no 6-minute re-execution needed, no risk of introducing a code regression into a verified run.

### The AI Partnership

> "AI is a fast, tireless data-wrangling engine. It will dry-run your entire pipeline before you approve the plan, catch a pandas-3 string-dtype subtlety in a traceback, and cross-check every Discussion number against executed output - but it needs you to set the judgment calls first (is 15 the right row threshold? is k=10 defensible? how much of the 11.1% outlier niche belongs in the recommendation?), and to verify the finished artifact afterward. The judgment layer stays human; the speed multiplies."

That pattern held throughout this project. The AI produced the parsers, the dry-runs, the fill script, and the verification pass; human approval locked the three judgment parameters (row threshold 15, n_components 30, k_final 10) before a single cell executed; and the two memory-induced number errors became a usable rule for the next notebook: *quote the line, or rewrite the sentence.*

### Final Thoughts

This project is 90% data wrangling wearing an ML costume - and the wrangling is exactly where unsupervised analysis succeeds or fails. The k-means on 30 principal components is the shortest part of the notebook; the 3.5 million sentinel codes, the bimodal missingness that made both thresholds landslides rather than guesses, and the `clean_data()` + row-threshold contract that puts customers and Germans on one identical footing - same 66 columns, same ≤15-missing-rows rule measured over one shared `QUALITY_COLUMNS` list (140,898 and 792,287 rows in the pipeline, 50,754 and 98,934 set aside as matching segments) - that is the deliverable.

The result is legible in a way unsupervised results often are not: two clusters holding half the customers from a fifth of the population, three clusters holding 3% of customers from a quarter of the population, and an inverse-transformed centroid that reads like a person (older, high-income, investor, green) rather than like a vector. The infrastructure detours - the unquoted `X` in a CSV, the seaborn-free image, the hue-normalization trap, the 30-second timeout vs. the 104-second sweep - were the unexpected part, and they are exactly where verification habits paid for themselves: probe the parser, time the loop, cross-check the prose. The lesson generalizes beyond this notebook: in AI-assisted work, the human sets the contract and verifies the artifact; the AI multiplies the speed, not the judgment.
